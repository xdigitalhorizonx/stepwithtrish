What happens at a flooring design consultation, and how to prepare

ProcessSeptember 8, 20266 min read

A flooring design consultation is a visit to your home, not a sales pitch: we measure the rooms, read your light and your subfloor, set samples on your floor, and leave with what we need to draw one plan and one quote. An hour of preparation makes it twice as useful.

What actually happens at a flooring consultation?

In one visit, we walk every room in the project, measure it, check what the existing floor and the subfloor are made of, and set samples on your floor in your own light. You talk; we listen for how the house is lived in. You end the visit knowing which two or three materials fit, and we leave with what we need to draw one plan and price the whole job once.

That is the shape of an in-home flooring consultation almost everywhere: measure, samples in your light, then a quote. What differs is what gets looked at between the measuring and the quote, and how much design thinking comes with it. Ours is an interior design consultation first and a flooring measure second, because the floor is the largest surface in the room and every other decision sits on it.

Here is what to expect, what to have ready, and two things every Nevada homeowner should check before hiring any contractor.

How should you prepare for a flooring consultation?

None of this involves a tape measure; that part is ours.

  • Photograph each room at two times of day, morning and late afternoon. Light is the first thing we ask about and the hardest thing to describe.
  • Decide the scope. Which rooms now, which rooms later, and whether the stairs are in. A floor that stops at a doorway is a design decision we would rather make on purpose.
  • Gather what you already love: a rug you own, a fabric, a paint chip, a saved photo. Two or three is plenty. It tells us your tone faster than any conversation.
  • Know how you live. Dogs, kids, a wheelchair, a piano, someone who walks barefoot at five in the morning. Each of those rules a material in or out.
  • Have a sense of what you want to spend, and say it early. It changes which samples come out of the bag, not how carefully we work.
  • Get everyone with a vote in the room. The samples only get set out once, in the light they will live in.
  • Tell us what you know about the floor's history: a past leak, a squeak, a slab crack, the vinyl under the carpet.

Why we read your light and your subfloor before the samples

You can change the floor. You cannot change the light or what is under it, so those come first. NOAA's comparative climatic data counts 251 days with sun a year at the Reno airport, 79% of the possible sunshine, and that light is hard and warm from the west by four in the afternoon. A sample that looked calm in a warehouse reads orange in a west-facing Somersett living room, which is why samples come to your house: the color has to be chosen under the light it will live in.

The subfloor decides the prep and half the material list. A wood subfloor gets metered: the National Wood Flooring Association's guidance is that hardwood and a wood subfloor should be within 4% moisture content of each other before installation, and that wood floors live best at 30% to 50% relative humidity and 60°F to 80°F, so we also ask how the house runs in winter. A concrete slab gets checked for flatness and moisture before anyone talks about glue-down planks. Old flooring gets identified, because what is under the carpet changes the removal. The sample bag opens after the floor has been read, not before.

From samples to one plan and one quote

By the end of the visit the field is down to two or three materials. What follows is drawing, not selling. The plan sets the direction of the boards or the tile, the pattern if there is one, where every transition falls and how the floors meet at the doorways, how the stairs are handled, and where the rugs go so the fade is planned rather than discovered. It is one plan for the whole floor, which is the point of doing design and installation with the same people.

The quote follows the plan, room by room, with the subfloor preparation on its own line. What moves it is the material grade and plank size, the square footage, how much the subfloor needs, stairs and transitions, and pattern. Compare it with another estimate on scope: is removal and haul-away included, is prep included, is furniture moving included, is there an acclimation plan, and who is the installer of record. A lower total that leaves those out is not lower. Our flooring installation page walks through what a complete scope covers.

How do you check a flooring contractor in Nevada?

Two checks, both free and both online. First, the license. The Nevada State Contractors Board states that all businesses or individuals who construct or alter any building must be licensed, with only a narrow exemption for small repair and maintenance work. Under the Nevada Administrative Code, flooring falls under classification C-16, Finishing floors, whose C-16a subclassification covers carpet, fabrics and similar materials but excludes ceramic tile and marble; tile work is classification C-20, Tiling. Search the Board's license lookup by business name or license number before you sign anything, and make sure the classification matches the work.

Second, the permit. Washoe County's building department lists interior finish work such as painting, wallpapering, tiling, carpeting, cabinets and countertops among the jobs that do not require a building permit. That is the county's list; if your home is inside Reno or Sparks city limits, confirm with that city's building department, since city rules can differ from the county's. A floor that is part of a larger remodel, or involves moving plumbing or structure, is a different conversation.

What the consultation is not

It is not a pitch. There is no price that expires when we leave and no pressure to decide in the room. It is not a measure-only visit either; a measure without design thinking gives you an accurate number for the wrong floor. And it is not a decision. You get the plan and the quote in writing, you take the time you need, and when you say go, we schedule.

We work this way across Reno, Sparks and the rest of Northern Nevada. If you would like to start, tell us about the space and when you would like it finished.

Takeaways

  1. A consultation is measure, samples in your light, then one plan and one quote.
  2. Prepare with photos at two times of day, the things you already love, and everyone who gets a vote.
  3. Light and subfloor come first because you cannot change them.
  4. Compare estimates by scope, not by the bottom line.
  5. Check any Nevada contractor on the State Contractors Board license search: flooring is C-16, tile is C-20.

Before you book: questions we are asked

Will replacing my floors need a building permit in Reno or Sparks?

Washoe County's building department lists interior finish work such as tiling and carpeting among the jobs that do not require a building permit. If your home is inside Reno or Sparks city limits, confirm with that city's building department, since city rules can differ from the county's.

How do I check whether a Nevada flooring contractor is licensed?

Search the Nevada State Contractors Board's license lookup by business name or license number. The Board states that anyone who constructs or alters a building must be licensed, with only a narrow exemption for small repair and maintenance work. Flooring is classification C-16 and tile is C-20.

What is included in a flooring estimate, and how should I compare them?

A complete estimate covers the material, removal and disposal of the old floor, subfloor preparation, installation, transitions and trim, and moving furniture, each stated plainly. Compare estimates line by line on scope; a lower total that leaves out prep or haul-away is not lower.
