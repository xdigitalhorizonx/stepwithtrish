The problem

Where does tile belong in a Reno home?

In the rooms that get wet or dirty, and any room you want cool in July and warm over radiant heat in January. In Reno and Sparks the mudroom argument is simple: snow, road grit and salt come in on boots for much of the winter, and porcelain does not care; its water absorption is 0.5 percent or less by definition, so a puddle at the door is a mop, not a repair.

Tile is not the answer everywhere. It is hard and cold without heat beneath it, it carries sound, and a floor that is not flat shows it as lippage. For a bedroom or a family room we would rather show you hardwood. The tile is the easy part; the layout, the substrate and the grout are the floor.

What you get

What you get with a Step With Trish tile floor

Material, format, pattern, grout and the surface beneath, decided together and drawn before anything is cut.

Porcelain, ceramic or natural stone, chosen for the room

Porcelain is dense, hard and nearly impervious, so it takes floors, showers, entries and dog nails without complaint. Ceramic is softer and easier to cut, at home on walls and lighter-traffic floors. Honed travertine, limestone and marble bring real veining and warmth and ask for sealing and a gentler cleaner.

Format and pattern that shape the room

Large-format porcelain with tight joints reads as one calm surface and makes a small bathroom feel wider. Zellige throws light around a kitchen. Herringbone, basketweave and checkerboard give an entry a floor with a point of view. We set the pattern to the room's proportions and the sightlines from the door.

Grout as a design decision

Joint width and grout color change a floor more than the tile does; a matched grout makes a field disappear, a contrasting one draws the pattern. High-performance grouts resist stains and do not need sealing, so a white kitchen floor stays white.

Radiant heat, planned from the start

Tile conducts heat well and does not mind the cycling, which makes it the natural floor over electric mats or hydronic tubing, and in a Northern Nevada house the heating season is long. We plan the heat with the layout so the mats sit where feet land.

The step

How a tile floor comes together

Three steps, the same people at each one.

  1. Step 01

    Consult

    We come to the house with samples, look at the room in its own light, check what the floor is built on and how flat it runs, and ask about radiant heat, showers and the dog. Then we narrow to two or three tiles.

  2. Step 02

    Design

    Tile, format, pattern, grout color and joint width are settled in the room against the vanity, the cabinets and the paint. The layout is drawn so cuts land where they are least seen and the pattern centers on the door or the tub. Interior design folds in when the whole room is changing.

  3. Step 03

    Install

    Substrate flattened and, where the room needs it, uncoupled or waterproofed. Heat mats laid and tested. Tiles set to the drawn lines with leveling clips on large formats, grouted, stone sealed. The floor is finished the way it was drawn.

What changes the price

What changes the price of a tile floor

The quote comes after the layout is drawn, because the layout decides the cuts, the waste and the hours far more than the tile does.

  1. 01

    Material and format

    Natural stone and handmade tile sit above standard porcelain; large formats cost more per piece, and rectified edges allow tighter joints but ask for a flatter floor.

  2. 02

    Pattern and layout

    Herringbone, basketweave and diagonal layouts take more cuts and more waste than a straight or offset lay, and borders and inlays are set piece by piece.

  3. 03

    Substrate, waterproofing and heat

    Leveling, uncoupling membranes, shower waterproofing and radiant mats or tubing all add layers before the first tile. How we prepare a floor explains what we look for.

  4. 04

    Grout, edges and transitions

    High-performance grouts cost more than cement grout and repay it in a kitchen. Bullnose, metal edge profiles, stair nosings and transitions into wood or carpet are each fitted by hand.

Questions

Questions Reno homeowners ask about tile

Is porcelain tile waterproof?

Effectively, yes; by the industry definition a porcelain tile absorbs 0.5 percent or less of its weight in water, which is why it is used in showers, entries and mudrooms. The floor as a whole is only as waterproof as its grout and the substrate beneath, so wet rooms still need a membrane under the tile.

What is the difference between porcelain and ceramic tile?

Porcelain is a denser, harder ceramic fired at a higher temperature, with water absorption of 0.5 percent or less; standard ceramic is softer, absorbs more water and is easier to cut. Porcelain suits floors, showers and entries; ceramic is at home on walls and lighter-traffic floors. Both are colorfast in sun.

What is the best flooring for a mudroom with snow and salt?

Porcelain tile, with a textured or matte finish for grip and a stain-resistant grout, because snowmelt, salt and grit do nothing to it. Luxury vinyl plank is the softer, warmer alternative if the mudroom opens onto a living space, and radiant heat under tile stops the room feeling like a garage.

Is a herringbone pattern more expensive than a straight lay?

Yes; a herringbone floor takes more cuts, more waste and more layout time than a straight or offset lay, so both material and labor rise. We draw the pattern first so you see what it does to the room before you commit.

Why is subfloor preparation important?

Because tile is rigid and does not forgive movement or hollows: a substrate that flexes cracks grout and tile, and one that is not flat shows as lippage. Preparation means checking deflection, flattening, and adding an uncoupling or waterproofing membrane where the room needs one.
